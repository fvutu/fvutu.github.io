Hitting 49% more clicks with two lines of copy.

The Weekly Job Summary is a core lifecycle email reaching 550K+ employers per week. It drives ~1,700 lifecycle-spender conversions per month across upsell and reactivation streams. But according to UXR, employers wanted a more action-oriented and personalized subject line.

So we built an A/B test variant incorporating both.

I led the subject line and preheader, from feedback synthesis to the final test copy.

what employers asked for

Likelihood-to-open was low. A concrete subject line would earn the open, not a generic "weekly update."

workshopping the line

Feedback
Employers
Prefer a subject line that includes a problem or something they need to take action on (e.g. 6 candidates waiting)
Claude
Personalization boosts open rate; numbers and specifics outperform vague language
SENIOR PM
Personalization aligns with direction from leadership
UXD
Use “jobs,” not “roles”; be careful with using “perform”
Current
Weekly Job Summary
Ideas from the team
⚠️ Your weekly job status — recommended next steps inside
Advised not to use the warning icon
Hiring update: Here’s where you stand and what to do next
Too many alts to choose: Job update, jobs update, job post updates
This week in hiring at {Company Name}: results + next steps
Personalized, but not yet right
{Company Name}, check in on your job status
My idea

The neutral or better winner criteria were the answer, because if habit kept open rates flat, the change still rolled out as a CX improvement. Later, the results showed employers weren’t confused.

…and the preheader

Our international lifecycle marketing manager offered 3 alternatives, each giving a preview of what to expect from the email. I pulled the most important bits from each into one.

Track your job activity and posts on Indeed
Catch up on your applications and key insights
Job performance and recommended next steps are inside
MY VERSION
Catch up on job activity, key insights, and next steps

what changed

Only the subject line and preheader differed, while the email body stayed the same.
The test adds the two elements employers asked for.

Those who opened the email were far likelier to click. A clearer, action-oriented subject line set accurate expectations of what's inside. Catchier subjects were usually risky at Indeed, but here, it resulted in more conversions.

View candidates rose 4-5x the other main CTAs. The test specifically pushed more employers to review their applicants, the action “check in on your job status” invites. The framing delivered on its promise.

setting new defaults in my first month

Each lift was steady with a palpable business impact: 49% more clicks per email. My versions are now the default for Weekly Job Summary campaigns.

Throughout my work, you’ll find me as the first or founding content designer and teaching partners how to work with me. Indeed didn't have email-specific content design, so I made an email-specific framework. The Weekly Job Summary was the most significant campaign I partnered on, and altogether they formed how I structured Indeed’s email content framework, then trained its internal content MCP.

Rethinking social relationships while still sounding human.

Friendships are what make Roblox, Roblox. With 73% under 18, users stay for the friends they’ve made and the identities they’ve formed.

However, parents worried bad actors had unfettered access to their kids via the Friends system. To make the platform safer, Roblox explored a social overhaul: Friends become Connections, with Trusted Connections as an age-verified layer for close friends.

I led content design on the initial rename and made the early case for Friends.

I was the first content designer on the project, defining early framing and content direction.
After I left, the feature was relaunched with final copy by the team.

understanding the terms

The team thought “Friends” didn't accurately reflect the levels of trust in a network, so this update was meant to be more flexible and safety-conscious. As the first content designer, I evaluated the naming system while advocating for language that matched users’ mental models.

And as a lifelong gamer, I tapped into my personal passion to speak to them—game devs, content creators, players of all ages.

friends
Two people agree to be Friends, like in real life. Standard across other gaming and social platforms.
They could
  • Send and receive messages, Party invites
  • Join each other's private games
  • Or remove each other
connections
Would replace Friends, with the same features seen through the lens of connection.
THEY CAN
  • Do everything Friends could, under a new name
trusted connections
A person’s inner circle, like Close Friends elsewhere. Both people must be age-checked, 13+.
They unlock
  • Unfiltered chat and voice chat in parties
  • Live collab on games with Team Create (Roblox Studio)
  • Content made by Trusted Connections and game customization
launching alongside

Voice & chat

SAFETY & MODERATION
All chat moderated for severe violations

This still led to the biggest risk: backlash to Connections.

the trouble with connections

The name was a fit on paper, but content design had neither presence nor input in research. The team prepped for launch and only asked for copy changes when I joined, but I pushed back. "Connections" didn’t match established social identity, and Friends + Close Friends were staples on gaming platforms.

The change risked making core interactions feel abstract, institutional, and emotionally distant.

My recommendation wasn't adopted before launch. In July 2025, the rename launched as-is.

They don’t manage connections—they make friends.

an unsuccessful launch

Connections + Trusted Connections launched to significant backlash. From dev forums to YouTube, Roblox players asked why Friends was replaced. In attempting to bridge the safety gap, it brought feelings of disconnect, distrust, and disregard.

They feared Roblox was becoming LinkedIn.


Scroll →

growing pains in a mature org

1 → 4
The content design team quadrupled in six months

After launch, our biggest hurdle was changing how the org thought about language.

Content design was new to most partners. Some welcomed us; others stayed skeptical until we’d built up wins across the product. I spent a lot of time explaining my job, like walking through my process, setting standards from scratch, and building reusable frameworks.

the case for everyday language

Across product, legal, and safety, formal language was the safe choice. I made the opposite case with every partner, that the words people already use are the ones they understand, trust, and act on. Everyday language is how safety actually lands, and every win we had made it easier to bring back Friends.

round 2 / trusted friends

The issue was always Connections—too formal, too detached, too unlike Roblox to its audience of millions.

Strategy

just bring back friends

It still aligned with safety initiatives, the new voice and tone, and user mental models.

New naming system
Same as Trusted Connections, with added parental controls
Not a proper noun
MORE CONTROLS FOR PARENTS
Connected parents are their child’s trusted friends, automatically
Trusted friends can be siblings, cousins, and IRL friends, approved by parents first

how it landed

friends were back
The term players used, from the beginning, had returned.
roblox was listening
Many saw the change as their voices finally being heard.

After years of unpopular platform decisions, players saw proof that their voice could change the product.

empathy in practice, not just principle

Product language profoundly shapes emotional perception and trust, especially in social platforms where certain terms carry cultural and behavioral meaning. Safety-driven systems still need to sound human, and users still need to feel heard.

Successful platform language must balance business goals with how users naturally describe themselves and their relationships.

Making content systems the foundation for an assistant.

Builders—Roblox employees, basically—lacked quick, reliable support for writing UX content. Our nascent content design team couldn’t scale to review everything ourselves, and we needed to empower non-content designers to make high-quality, consistent copy on their own.

Meanwhile, the design org was rapidly innovating an AI design system with an internal companion, Design Assist, for Figma and Slack.

We saw an opportunity for content design functionality in Design Assist, and to treat content systems (voice, tone, heuristics) as infrastructure other AI tools could build on. With non-content designers empowered to write copy themselves, we’d be free to focus on strategic work.

I led the foundational work and designed how the assistant would put them to use.

a voice without guidelines

The first step was training the assistant on content guidelines, but we didn't have any.

Roblox's voice and tone did exist, for brand. It just didn't translate into how the product should actually speak. And since we didn't have years of content design docs to draw from, we adapted the brand voice and tone.

We prioritized the surfaces Builders wrote for most (buttons, errors, upsells) and foundations (voice, empathy, style). I owned the foundational guidelines.

Bringing short-form content discovery back to Roblox.

Players always captured their gameplay, but they left Roblox to share it. On YouTube, viewers clocked 1.5 billion minutes of Roblox content a day, and #roblox was one of TikTok’s fastest-growing hashtags. On Roblox itself, beyond playing and customizing avatars, players had little outlet to create.

Developers felt it too. Promoting a game through authentic, player-made content was difficult, and discovery felt stagnant. They needed better paths to reach new players.

Moments fix that: clips up to 30 seconds, swipeable feed. One tap takes you from watching to playing.

1.5B
min of Roblox videos watched daily, on YouTube

I led end-to-end content design for creation, safety, and consumption.

landing on moments

Internally, the project was called Rodeo (Roblox Video). The team oscillated on what to call them—Clips, Highlights, Rodeos? Leading content design here, with no naming system or semantic hierarchy to lean on, I had to create one.

"Moments" came naturally; gameplay videos were the engaging moments players shared with friends. Distinctive and memorable for a mass audience.

Innovating personalized chat customization for CX.

The tone menu in Qualtrics Assist’s original design wasn't wrong, but it just wasn’t ours.

Witty. Confident. Empathetic. Six options handed to users that never once looked to Qualtrics for wit. Our users were CX professionals at major companies, seeking deeper insights from customer feedback. Why would they choose a tone we didn’t have?

Other assistants offered options to customize tone, length, and formality. I saw the potential for personalization and later, to align the tool to users' brand voice.

witty
Didn't match a product built for serious, professional work
confident
Implies its opposite exists elsewhere; a bad signal for a tool meant to help people trust their own data
personable, direct
Voice traits masquerading as tone
empathetic
Only one that held up, but it's standard

I led end-to-end design across
content, conversation, and interaction.

The literature backed up a pattern I kept seeing anecdotally: an AI with no defined character undermines trust about as fast as one that oversells its own abilities (Dorton & Harper, 2022)Dorton, S.
